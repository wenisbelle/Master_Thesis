GA CONVERGENCE ANALYSIS - Analytical (egocentric and swarm)
============================================================

Problem observed
----------------
In ga_logbook.txt (egocentric/ and swarm/), the minimum cost appears very early
and then barely improves, or even gets worse (swarm: 577k at gen 9 -> 612k at
gen 20). A GA is expected to keep improving over the generations.

Short answer
------------
The GA is not converging to a good minimum. It stops being able to search after
about 8 generations, and from then on the "min" column is mostly measurement
noise. There are three causes, all in the GA setup of egocentric/main.py (the
same setup is in swarm/main.py).


1. There is almost no mutation, so the GA can only reshuffle its starting values
-------------------------------------------------------------------------------

    MUTATION_PROBABILITY = 0.05                  # chance an offspring is mutated
    toolbox.register("mutate", ..., indpb=0.05)  # chance each gene of that offspring is mutated

These two probabilities multiply. With 7 genes, about 1.5% of offspring get any
mutation at all, which is roughly 0.9 gene changes per generation, or about 17
in the whole run (swarm, 10 genes: about 25).

Everything else comes from cxTwoPoint. That operator only swaps gene values
between parents and never creates a new number. So the GA can only try
combinations of the 50 random values drawn for each gene in generation 0.
Tournament selection then removes the weaker values. By generation 8-10 most of
the population is made of the same few values, and there is nothing new left to
test. That is exactly where both curves go flat.

Generations 0-8 do show real progress: the average fell from 273k to 150k
(egocentric) and from 1.6M to 660k (swarm). That is the GA picking the best mix
of its starting values.


2. After that, selection is mostly picking lucky runs
-----------------------------------------------------

Each fitness is the average of 3 stochastic runs (per swarm size), and one
individual can range from 52k to 93k in a single run. If you take 50 nearly
identical individuals with noisy fitness, the expected minimum is about

    avg - 2.25 * std

(2.25 is the expected minimum of 50 draws from a standard normal distribution).
Comparing that prediction with the logbooks:

    gen | egocentric               | swarm
        | avg-2.25*std  logged min | avg-2.25*std  logged min
    ----+--------------------------+--------------------------
     10 |     108k        117k     |     599k        577k
     15 |     112k        114k     |     592k        597k
     20 |     105k        116k     |     610k        612k

(Egocentric gen 14 is left out: one individual with dead drones, cost 9.4e7,
inflated the std and makes the prediction negative.)

Noise alone explains almost all of the late "min" values. The Hall-of-Fame
results (111,892 egocentric and 577,449 swarm) are most likely the luckiest of
about 1,000 evaluations, not the best parameters. A fresh evaluation of them
will probably come out closer to the population average.

Smaller noise problem: the wind uses numpy's global random generator
(np.random.normal in gradysim battery_model.py). Python's "random" module is
reseeded automatically in each new worker process, but numpy's is not. So the 16
pool workers very likely all produce the same wind sequence, which means the
noise is not independent between individuals.


3. There is no elitism, and old fitness values are never re-measured
--------------------------------------------------------------------

eaSimple does not keep the best individual. It also only evaluates new or
changed individuals, so a copy that survives unchanged keeps its old, possibly
lucky score. That is why the swarm "min" gets worse (577k -> 612k): the lucky
individual was crossed over and lost.


Minor issues
------------
- Linear scale for range-type parameters. base_variance (0.25-100),
  energy_gamma (0.1-100) and distance_between_drone_norm (10-1000) are drawn
  uniformly, so about 90% of samples fall above 10% of the range. If a good
  value is small, it is almost never tried. Sampling these on a log scale
  would fix that.
- Small budget. 50 x 20 evaluations is small for a noisy problem with 7-10
  continuous parameters.


WHAT TO DO (in order of impact)
===============================

A. Use bounded real-valued operators and a real mutation rate
-------------------------------------------------------------
These operators stay inside the bounds, so the clamp decorator is no longer
needed:

    LOW  = [low  for _, low, high in GENE_BOUNDS]
    HIGH = [high for _, low, high in GENE_BOUNDS]
    toolbox.register("mate", tools.cxSimulatedBinaryBounded, low=LOW, up=HIGH, eta=15)
    toolbox.register("mutate", tools.mutPolynomialBounded, low=LOW, up=HIGH, eta=20,
                     indpb=1.0 / len(GENE_BOUNDS))
    MUTATION_PROBABILITY = 0.3

B. Seed every run explicitly, same seeds for every individual of a generation
----------------------------------------------------------------------------
This technique is called "common random numbers". The cost differences between
individuals then reflect their parameters, not the wind or start positions they
happened to get. It also fixes the shared numpy generator in the workers.

    # create_and_run_simulation(..., seed=None):
    if seed is not None:
        random.seed(seed); np.random.seed(seed)

    # objective_function(individual, seeds):
    #   seed = seeds[r] + number_of_drones  for run r

C. Own generation loop with elitism, re-evaluating everyone each generation
--------------------------------------------------------------------------
It costs about 20% more evaluations (about 42 of 50 are already evaluated each
generation). In exchange, lucky scores do not stick and elites are always
measured on the current seeds:

    from functools import partial
    ELITE_SIZE = 2
    for gen in range(1, NUMBER_OF_GENERATIONS + 1):
        seeds = [random.randrange(2**31) for _ in range(RUNS_PER_EVALUATION)]  # new scenarios each generation
        elites = [toolbox.clone(e) for e in tools.selBest(pop, ELITE_SIZE)]
        offspring = algorithms.varAnd(toolbox.select(pop, len(pop) - ELITE_SIZE),
                                      toolbox, CROSSOVER_PROBABILITY, MUTATION_PROBABILITY)
        pop = elites + offspring
        for ind, fit in zip(pop, toolbox.map(partial(toolbox.evaluate, seeds=seeds), pop)):
            ind.fitness.values = fit
        hof.update(pop)
        log.record(gen=gen, nevals=len(pop), **stats.compile(pop))

D. Validate the final answer
----------------------------
Re-evaluate the top 5-10 individuals with 20-30 fresh seeds, and choose by mean
cost with a confidence interval. Report that number in the thesis, not the GA's
"min". This also tells whether the search is on a genuine plateau: if all the
top candidates are statistically tied, the remaining improvement is small
whatever optimizer is used.

E. Smaller fixes
----------------
- Encode the range-type parameters on a log scale.
- Log a per-gene diversity measure, to see when the population collapses.
- Add the median to the stats, since the dead-drone outliers distort avg and std.
- Run more generations once A-C are in place.

F. Alternative optimizer
------------------------
CMA-ES (deap.cma.Strategy, already in DEAP) usually does better than a simple GA
on noisy continuous problems like this one. A population of 16 would match the
16 cores.

Recommended starting point: A-C together, since they target all three causes.
